Most leaders don't need a new system. They need a weekly target clear enough to make decisions against.

One sentence can do that. Written down before the week starts, it becomes a filter for every meeting, every request, and every emotional spike that shows up between Monday and Friday.

Define success before the week defines it for you.

## The Problem

When success is undefined, something else defines it. Usually distraction.

The first email of the week sets the agenda. The loudest person in the building sets the priority. The most urgent problem, which is rarely the most important one, eats Tuesday and Wednesday. By Thursday you're tired, and you can't quite say what the week was for.

**Undefined success always gets replaced by random urgency.**

That isn't a character flaw. It's physics. A week with no target will be pulled toward whatever has the most gravity in the moment, and the moment almost never cares about your mission.

The cost compounds. Your team watches where your attention goes and assumes that's the priority. If your attention is reactive, their work becomes reactive. Alignment erodes one reasonable-sounding interruption at a time.

## What It Actually Looks Like

Picture two leaders walking into the same Monday.

The first has nothing written down. They open the inbox, clear what's on fire, take the meetings already on the calendar, and say yes to three new requests because each one seems fair on its own. Friday afternoon, they've worked fifty hours and couldn't tell you in one sentence what got won.

The second wrote one line at 7:00 AM: *A successful week looks like the capital budget draft in the finance committee's hands by Thursday.*

Same inbox. Same fires. Same requests. But every one of them now gets measured against something. Does this move the budget draft? If not, it waits, gets delegated, or gets a no.

That leader isn't working harder. They're working against a target.

I run operations for an organization with more than 160 employees and a real estate portfolio north of $15 million. On any given Monday there are a dozen legitimate things that could consume the whole week. Most of them are good. Very few are the thing.

The weekly sentence is how I tell the difference. It doesn't make the other work disappear. It puts it in the right order.

**A written definition of success does three things at once:**

- **It creates focus.** You stop grading the week by how full it felt.
- **It sharpens alignment.** When your team knows the sentence, they stop guessing what matters to you.
- **It improves decision quality.** Every yes and no now has a reason attached.

The sentence has to be specific. "Make progress on strategy" is not a target. It's a mood. "Finalize the three priorities for Q4 and communicate them to department heads by Wednesday" is a target. You'll know by Friday whether you hit it.

## The Wisdom Underneath

Proverbs says the plans of the diligent lead to plenty. Diligence there isn't about hours. It's about having a plan in the first place.

## Where to Start

**First, run the diagnostic right now.** Could you explain this week's win in one sentence? Not your task list. The win. If you can't answer in ten seconds, the week is already being defined for you.

**Second, make it a Monday ritual.** Before you open email, write one line: *A successful week looks like…* Finish the sentence with an outcome someone else could verify. Keep it where you'll see it: top of your notebook, first line of your task app, a sticky note on the monitor.

**Third, share it.** Tell your assistant, your leadership team, or your direct reports. A sentence you keep to yourself guides you. A sentence your team knows guides everyone. It also gives them permission to protect your time from things that don't serve it.

Then on Friday, look back at the sentence and grade it honestly. Hit or miss. No partial credit for effort.

Do that for a month and you'll notice something. The weeks start feeling shorter and the results start getting bigger.

Clarity isn't a personality trait. It's a practice, and it starts with one sentence written before the noise gets a vote.
