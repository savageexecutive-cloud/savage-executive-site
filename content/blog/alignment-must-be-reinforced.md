Ask every leader on your team to write down the top priority for this quarter. Don't coach them. Don't clarify. Just collect the answers and compare them.

If you get one voice, you have alignment. If you get seven versions, you have an announcement you mistook for alignment.

Most leaders assume alignment because they remember making the announcement. The team feels alignment when they can repeat the priority in the same language and act on it without hesitation.

## The Problem With "I Already Told Them"

I hear a version of this constantly: *"We covered that at the off-site."* Or, *"I sent the email."* Or, *"It's in the strategic plan."*

Here's the uncomfortable truth. One announcement is not communication. It's the start of communication.

Leaders overestimate how clearly they communicate priorities. You said it once, from the front of the room, with slides and momentum and your own conviction filling in every gap. You walked away believing the room walked away with what you walked away with.

They didn't. They walked away with their fragment of it, filtered through their own department, their own pressure, their own unfinished projects.

**Alignment is not assumed. It's reinforced.** Through repetition, through shared language, through operational rhythm. Skip that work and the fog rolls back in within a week.

## What It Actually Looks Like

I've watched this play out inside healthy organizations — teams hitting their numbers, ministries growing, staff working hard and long.

The symptoms are quiet. A department pours a quarter into something adjacent to the main thing, because the main thing was never named clearly enough to repeat. Two leaders make opposite calls in the same week and both believe they're serving the priority. A meeting produces energy but no decision, because everyone in the room is optimizing for a slightly different outcome.

None of these people are lazy or resistant. They're unaligned. And unaligned effort is the most expensive kind — because it *looks* like progress right up until the quarter closes and nothing moved.

The test of clarity is not what the leader thinks was said. It's what the team repeats when the leader isn't in the room.

## Repetition Is a Discipline, Not a Weakness

Somewhere leaders picked up the idea that repeating yourself is a communication failure. That if you were clear enough the first time, you wouldn't have to say it again.

Backwards. Repetition is how priorities become instinct.

Teams need to hear the same language, in the same words, enough times that they stop coaching themselves toward it and start acting from it automatically. That takes far more repetition than any leader is comfortable with. If you're tired of saying it, your team is probably just beginning to hear it.

Eleven years of running budgets under target taught me that the organizations that execute aren't the ones with the best plan. They're the ones where everyone can say the plan back to you the same way. Clarity that only lives in the leader's head governs nothing.

## Where to Start

You don't fix this with another retreat. You fix it with rhythm.

**1. Name the one priority in a single sentence.** Not the OKR list. One outcome the team can quote under pressure. If it takes a paragraph, it isn't clear yet — keep cutting until it fits in a breath.

**2. Reinforce it on a rhythm, not a whim.** Open your next team meeting by restating the top priority in one sentence. Do it the meeting after that. Build it into the update, the one-on-one, the standing agenda. Reinforcement that depends on you remembering will fade; reinforcement built into the operating rhythm survives your busy weeks.

**3. Ask someone else to repeat it.** This is the move most leaders skip because it feels awkward. Don't ask, "Does that make sense?" Ask a specific person to say the priority back in their own words. The gap between what you said and what they can repeat is the exact gap that's costing you execution — and you cannot close a gap you refuse to measure.

## The Closing Frame

The dangerous question isn't whether *you* know the priority. It's whether your team would answer it with one voice or seven versions.

Clarity isn't a speech you give once. It's a discipline you reinforce until the language becomes shared instinct — until the team can carry the priority into every room you're not standing in.

Say it again. Then ask them to say it back. That's not over-communicating. That's leadership.
