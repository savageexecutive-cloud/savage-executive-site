Most leaders only notice margin after it's gone.

By then it's too late. The surprise has already landed — a key person quits, a major client pays late, a roof needs replacing — and there's no room to absorb it. The decision gets made from panic instead of from strength.

Here's what I've come to believe after years of watching leaders operate under pressure: margin is not spare space for weak leaders. It's a weapon. The leader with room can move while everyone else is frozen.

## The Problem With Running Tight

We've been taught to admire the maxed-out leader. Calendar packed wall to wall. Every dollar deployed. Every ounce of energy spent. It looks like commitment. It looks like efficiency.

It's actually fragility dressed up as discipline.

When you run that tight, you lose the one thing leadership actually requires: the ability to respond instead of react. A leader with no margin doesn't make decisions — the decisions get made *for* them by whatever crisis arrives first.

I've watched it happen the same way every time. The overloaded leader panics faster. They miss opportunities because they have no capacity to pursue them. And they react emotionally to problems that a rested, resourced leader would handle in five calm minutes.

Margin creates flexibility. Flexibility creates strength.

## What This Looks Like in the Real World

Picture two leaders facing the same bad week.

The first has slack in the budget, open space on the calendar, and gas left in the tank. When the surprise hits, they think. They weigh options. They negotiate from a position of patience because they don't *need* the deal to close today.

The second is operating at the redline in all three areas. Same surprise, completely different response. No cash cushion means every vendor conversation is a fire. No calendar room means there's no time to think it through. No energy reserve means the emotional fuse is already short.

Same problem. One leader has options. The other has only reactions.

When I ran operations for an organization with 160-plus employees and $15 million in real estate, the years that nearly broke us weren't the years revenue dropped. They were the years we had no reserves when it did. After we got disciplined about building margin first — and finished eleven straight years under budget — the surprises didn't stop coming. They just stopped being threats.

## Margin Is Infrastructure, Not Luxury

This is the reframe that changes everything: margin isn't what's left over. It's what you build first.

Reserves create options, and options create better decisions. That's true of money, but it's just as true of time and energy. A leader who guards a clear afternoon each week is building decision-making capacity the same way a CFO builds a cash reserve.

There's old wisdom here. The ant stores in summer for a reason — not because winter is certain to be brutal, but because reserves turn an uncertain winter into a survivable one. Stored capacity is what lets you say yes to the right opportunity and no to the wrong emergency.

## Where to Start

Don't try to build margin everywhere at once. That's how it never happens. Pick one form and build it first.

**Choose your weakest reserve.** Is it calendar, financial, or energy? Be honest about which one runs closest to empty. That's where the next surprise will hurt most.

**Build a small buffer there — and name it.** A line item in the budget. A protected block on the calendar. A hard stop on your week. Make it concrete enough that you'd notice if it disappeared.

**Then defend it like payroll.** This is the part most leaders skip. Margin you won't protect isn't margin — it's just slack waiting to be spent by the first person who asks. You don't raid payroll when things get busy. Treat your reserves the same way.

Here's the diagnostic question I'd leave you with: *Where am I operating so tight that even a small surprise now feels like a threat?*

Wherever that is — that's where you're fragile. And fragility is a choice you can start reversing this week.

The leader with margin isn't the one who works less. It's the one who, when the pressure comes, still has room to move.
